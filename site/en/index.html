Refuting Dovgoshey–Rovenska Conjecture 4.2, arXiv:2610.11596v1, Section 4, p. 13: sharp bounds and corollaries of Petrov (2022) and Dovgoshey et al. (2026)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

How few pairs of points can have distances available from every point in a finite collection? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang show that Dovgoshey and Rovenska’s proposed answer fails: when exactly two positive distances are available everywhere, hierarchical distance systems require more such pairs than general ones with the same number of points. They give exact replacement bounds.

In a distance table, each row records one point’s distances to all points. Numbers appearing in every row form the “center of distances”: numbers, not a central location. Zero qualifies because each point has distance zero from itself. Connect distinct points whose distance qualifies; this central graph counts unordered pairs, without self-connections. Ordinary distances obey the triangle rule: a direct route cannot exceed a two-step route. If objects sit in nested boxes, assign a larger distance when a larger box is needed to contain both. Every triangle’s two longest sides then tie; this stronger rule defines an ultrametric.

Take four points in two pairs, with distance one-half within pairs and one between pairs. This is an ultrametric; every point sees both positive distances, so all six pairs are central edges. Compare the number-line points 0, 4, 5 and 9. Every point sees distances four and five; one and nine are not available everywhere. Including zero, both centers contain three numbers, but the second example has only four central edges. The conjecture proposed two groups, one of size two, with all cross-group edges and, inside each group, edges from one chosen point to all others. The first example has that pattern, yet fails to minimize among all metrics.

Let n count the points, k the central distances including zero, and r be half of n rounded upward. Each positive central distance must have enough edges to touch every point. One edge touches only two points, so at least r are needed. Distinct distances use disjoint edges: the total is at least (k−1)r. The authors attain this for every possible n, k with n at least three. They build disjoint edge collections covering all points, using pairings and repairs for odd n. Give each collection its own length, at least one but less than two, and leftover edges individually unique lengths. Two lengths always sum to more than any third, ensuring the ordinary triangle rule; unique lengths miss some points. Thus three central distances need exactly 2r edges.

In an ultrametric, the largest distance separates points into groups, all cross-group pairs having that distance. With three central distances, each group needs at least two points to supply the other positive distance internally. For n at least four, counting gives at least 2n−4 cross-edges, plus r at the other distance. Groups of sizes two and n−2, internally arranged into pairs and sometimes a triple, attain 2n−4+r. Consequently no three-central ultrametric minimizes among all metrics. Even among ultrametrics, the conjecture’s pattern loses from six points onward: eleven edges suffice at six points, instead of twelve.

Another corrected conjecture concerns n points with n central distances: these exist exactly for a singleton or even n, not just sizes one, two and four. Every positive distance must pair each point with exactly one other point, forcing evenness; classical round-robin pairing schedules give the converse. Love (2023) and Bargetz and coauthors (2024) already supplied counterexamples and receive credit. The minimum point count for k central distances is one for k=1, k for even k, and k+1 for odd k greater than one.

Earlier Dovgoshey–Rovenska work gives the ultrametric minimum point count as 2^(k−1). Dovgoshey and coauthors classified equality by a balanced tree: each fork has two branches, leaves have equal depth, and distance labels depend only on depth. Two leaves’ distance is the label where their upward paths first meet. The present authors derive consequences from that classification. Independently swapping branches gives exactly 2^(n−1) distance-preserving permutations. Any point can move to any other, a property called homogeneity. Among homogeneous n-point ultrametrics this permutation count is smallest, with equality exactly for these minimum-size spaces. Around each point, counts at successive central distances are 1, 1, 2, 4, and so on, including the point itself first; these counts also characterize the minimum-size spaces.

Petrov (2022) receives credit for the earlier uniform-branching characterization behind the homogeneous-tree discussion. The manuscript reports Lean evidence only for the four-point refutation, not the general bounds or corollaries. Reported exact finite computations include edge constructions for four through thirty-two points; all-size conclusions rely on written proofs. Beyond binary trees, the authors characterize sphere counts for homogeneous ultrametrics: the list starts with one, and each cumulative count divides the next. This is only a partial settlement: arbitrary ultrametrics may have additional, noncentral distances, and their general sphere-count question remains open.
